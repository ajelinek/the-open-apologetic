Have you ever watched a crime show where two witnesses give exactly the same story? The detective asks questions, and both witnesses describe the event in perfect agreement. Every detail matches. Every word lines up.

That makes for good television. But in real life, it almost never happens.

This is something that puzzles a lot of people when they read the four Gospels. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John all tell the story of Jesus' resurrection. But they do not agree on every detail. Different writers mention different women at the tomb. They describe different times of day. They see different beings inside the tomb.

If you are looking for the story to match perfectly, this might seem like a problem. Many people assume that if the Bible is true, all four accounts should read like the same transcript. But here is what is interesting. The differences actually work in the Bible's favor.

## What the Four Gospels Say

Let us look at what each Gospel writer records about the morning Jesus rose from the dead.

### Who went to the tomb

The Gospel writers do not agree on who visited the tomb that morning.

Matthew mentions two women: Mary Magdalene and "the other Mary." Mark adds more names. He says Mary Magdalene went, along with Mary the mother of James and Salome. Luke expands the group even further. He lists Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and "the others with them." John initially mentions only Mary Magdalene, though other women were likely present.

### When they arrived

The writers also describe different times of day.

Matthew says it happened "after the sabbath, as the first day was dawning." Mark writes that the women came "very early when the sun had risen." Luke says it was "at daybreak on the first day." John simply states "early, while it was still dark."

These are not contradictions. They are different ways of describing the same early morning. Someone walking to a tomb at dawn might describe it differently depending on how early they left and how far they traveled.

### What they saw inside

The descriptions of the angel or angels also vary.

Matthew describes an "angel of the Lord" who rolled back the stone. The guards at the tomb fainted from fear. Mark describes "a young man sitting on the right side, clothed in a white robe." Luke mentions "two men in dazzling garments." John records "two angels in white, sitting there, one at the head and one at the feet."

Different writers noticed different details. Different writers emphasized different aspects of what they saw. This is exactly what you would expect from real people describing a miraculous event.

## Why Differences Mean Authenticity

Here is where the puzzle becomes interesting. If the Gospel writers had invented this story, they would have coordinated their accounts. They would have made sure Mary Magdalene arrived at the same time in every Gospel. They would have described the same angel in the same way.

But they did not do that. And that is telling.

Legal professionals know this. They know that genuine eyewitnesses produce accounts with natural variations. False witnesses tend to rehearse their stories until they match perfectly. Variation suggests truth. Perfection suggests fabrication.

Television has warped our expectations. The "CSI Effect" describes how crime shows train viewers to expect forensic evidence and perfect witness agreement. In real life, that almost never happens. Real investigations rely on piecing together accounts that do not match exactly.

The Gospel writers give us exactly what real eyewitnesses would give us. They tell us what they saw. They do not try to match each other's stories.

## The Embarrassing Detail

There is another reason the differences matter. The Gospel writers all agree on one surprising detail: women were the first witnesses to the resurrection.

In the first-century Jewish world, some legal contexts placed less weight on women's testimony. If someone were inventing a story to make it more believable, they might have chosen male disciples as the first witnesses instead.

But the Gospel writers did not change this. They kept the women as the first witnesses even though it might have made their story harder to believe. Scholars call this an "embarrassing detail." It is the kind of thing you would expect to get edited out if someone were making up the story.

The fact that all four Gospels keep women as the first witnesses, while still differing on everything else, tells us something. The writers were not trying to create a polished narrative. They were trying to tell what happened. And what happened was that women went to the tomb first and found it empty.

## What This Means

The differences between the Gospel accounts are not flaws. They are features. They tell us that real people, with different perspectives and different memories, recorded what they saw.

No two people experience an event the same way. No two people tell a story identically. The Gospel writers were not writing identical transcripts. They were writing about what they had witnessed and what it meant to them.

When you read the four Gospels, you get four perspectives on the most important event in Christian history. You see four people trying to describe something miraculous. Their differences do not cancel out their testimony. They add to it.

The resurrection story is not worse for having four versions. It is better. It gives us a fuller picture. It shows us that these were real people telling real stories.

The question is not why the Gospels disagree. The question is why we expect them to agree perfectly when no real witnesses ever do.
