# ACTs Theology Talk — 2026

Hi, my name is Adam Jelinek. When I was asked to do a talk on theology, I was hesitant. To me, theology felt like something for professors, teachers, and Bible experts. The journey people take on this path can and should look different. Everyone is at a different chapter in their life, and you should not compare yourself to someone who is further along — we all have to start somewhere.

This is my story. Like any good story, it is a love story — one where one person loves the other unconditionally, and the other fights it and runs away until they finally give in and are changed forever.

## Meeting Jennifer

It all started when I met Jennifer. On our first “real” date she told me three rules: “I don’t date anyone I work with, anyone younger than me, and I will not date anyone who is not Catholic.” It’s funny thinking back. I can picture that first meeting plainly: we were in a work meeting, she wore a black knitted silk blouse, and I remember thinking, “Wow — I hope my wife looks that good when she’s that age.” (Yes, she is eight years older than me.)

The third rule was trickier. I was 100% agnostic — I didn’t go to church and didn’t believe in God. So what did I say?

## Life (background)

Before I answer, I need to tell you about my childhood. I considered myself an atheist. My dad and I would discuss our disbelief in God and how religion seemed like something created to control people. My mom was “religious” culturally; she’d been raised Catholic but stopped attending after a painful family incident involving a pregnancy out of wedlock.

We never openly prayed or discussed faith, religion, politics, or feelings. I can’t say I even knew who God was. My best friends were churchgoers; I went to church with one friend sometimes, partly because he asked and partly because of a girl who came from a devout Catholic family.

I don’t remember a single Mass, but I do remember two things besides the girl. One: a Sunday School question — “Is there ever a time you would steal?” The expected answer was “No,” but I said “Yes” if my family were starving and it was a matter of survival. I was told, “No — God would provide,” and I thought that was a poor answer.

Two: while helping set up a church event I saw men (a priest and a deacon, I later realized) drinking and smoking and having a good time. That took away some of the awe I had for religious leaders; they were regular people.

I also worked for a respected local businessman who once told me I should own my own business and that he had left Catholicism because it was a lie. At the time I didn’t understand his pain; he quoted Matthew 5:29 about removing what causes you to sin, and we discussed why that might seem hard to reconcile with ordinary life.

It wasn’t until college that I realized being an atheist with no proof either way was not a strong position — I had no evidence either way and was just moving toward a new perspective.

## The Rules and Church

Back to Jennifer’s third rule. We were at a local Outback, seated on a bench at the bar. My softening from atheist to agnostic, the loss of two girlfriends over faith differences, and honest conversations with college friends helped me craft an answer: I had no issue with the faith, and I wanted our kids to be raised in the Church because I believed it would help them.

When you’re agnostic everything falls on you. If things don’t go your way there’s no comfort in saying “God told me no” — it’s all on you. That answer satisfied Jennifer enough that we began dating officially. Six months later we were engaged and six months after that we were married. It may seem fast, but my family had similar quick courtships.

After we were engaged, Jennifer worried I wouldn’t go to church with her after we had kids. Lovestruck, I started attending Mass on Sundays.

## Early Church Experience

Jennifer started me gradually. The first Mass she took me to was an Easter Mass with Father Kritz — we were out in thirty minutes. Most Sundays we’d drive up to Wenona for a twenty-minute Mass with Father Kretz and then go to her mom’s for pancakes and bacon. The forty-five-minute round trip was worth the breakfast.

Father Kritz played a big role in our lives; he guided our marriage prep and married us. He knew I wasn’t Catholic. After one meeting he asked to meet with us individually: Jennifer first, then me. He said he wanted to educate me on why Catholicism was right.

Jennifer’s meeting left her upset — Father told her she couldn’t “return” me like a purchase and implied she shouldn’t marry me because I wasn’t Catholic. Later, when I met with him, I opened with, “I don’t understand how you can believe in a God.” He initially said, “If that is what you believe I can’t help you,” but then he introduced me to Pascal’s Wager.

## Pascal’s Wager

Pascal presented four scenarios: either God exists or not, and you either believe or do not. The outcomes range from infinite gain if God exists and you believe, to infinite loss if God exists and you refuse belief. If God does not exist, belief costs finite things at most. Pascal argued the rational choice is to believe because the potential gain outweighs the finite cost.

This idea kept me attending Mass for about seven years.

## The Turning Point: Alpha and RCIA

Around year seven I grew frustrated — I felt I was attending for Jennifer, praying for the kids for her, and the daily devotions were wearing on me. Jennifer asked if I would learn more about the Church to help answer our kids’ questions and suggested RCIA, but first there was a new program called ALPHA.

I was reluctant, but when she said there would be a movie and food with no kids, I agreed. The first ALPHA videos spoke about a hole inside us that nothing — alcohol, drugs, sex — can fill. Those words resonated deeply.

At the discussion table I stayed quiet because I didn’t want to upset Jennifer with my skepticism. Then Rich Sealy met with me one-on-one. He answered my questions — about Old Testament violence, about human nature, and about faith — with thoughtful, reasoned explanations rather than “just have faith.”

He explained how ancient people were in a primitive moral state and God met them where they were, educating people over time, much like a parent guiding a child.

Rich helped me reconcile difficult passages and challenged my simplistic views about others (for example, gay couples) by explaining Christian concepts of cross-bearing and moral responsibility.

He sent me on a path to seek evidence rather than take blind leaps.

## Evidence and Reason

I began watching TrueU videos and reading material that argued the universe shows evidence of design. Key points that influenced me:

- God created space and time, which implies God exists outside of them. That idea helped me conceptualize a timeless Creator.
- Many prominent scientists saw no conflict between science and faith; Kepler said scientific work is “thinking God’s thoughts after him.”
- Einstein’s cosmological constant and Hubble’s expansion pointed toward a beginning (the Big Bang).
- Dr. Meyer referenced the Penrose number to show the improbability of a universe formed by chance (on the order of 1 in 10^(10^123)).
- Arguments from information theory and molecular biology: the odds of a functional protein forming by chance are astronomically small (e.g., 1 in 10^164 for a modest-length protein), which appealed to me as a software engineer by analogy to code and information.

These arguments didn’t prove Scripture, but they shifted my view that the universe likely had a Designer.

## Historical Reliability of the Bible

I also studied the historical reliability of the Bible. Compared to other ancient works, the Bible has a wealth of manuscripts: thousands of New Testament Greek manuscripts and tens of thousands of ancient copies overall, some dating only decades after the originals. Scholars estimate the New Testament is about 99.5% textually pure, with the small remainder consisting of spelling, word order, or minor differences that don’t affect doctrine.

By contrast, many classical texts survive in far fewer copies with larger gaps.

Reading C.S. Lewis further challenged me. Lewis argued you can’t reasonably accept Jesus as merely a great moral teacher while rejecting His claims to divinity; the options are startling and force a decision.

## RCIA and Becoming Catholic

During RCIA, halfway through, Rich told me I would be joining the Church and needed to pick a patron saint. That shook me. I called an agnostic friend for breakfast to talk me down; I ended up eating a Chick-fil-A chicken biscuit and deciding I was going to join.

Around then my grandfather was hospitalized. I drove twelve hours to Kansas and, unsure how to pray, noticed signs and street names for St. Joseph repeatedly. I prayed for St. Joseph’s intercession that night; my grandfather’s breathing improved and we slept through the night.

Easter 2016, I was baptized and confirmed into the Church. It was the most beautiful Easter Mass I’ve attended.

## Resurrection (community and change)

Nicky Gumbel in the ALPHA series talked about “porridge parties” and how odd they felt before joining the Church. I understood that: before, I thought many church people were weird, but now they are the people I want closest to me — my friends and family.

Jim Rohn says, “You are the average of the five people you spend the most time with.” Proverbs 13:20 says, “Walk with the wise and you become wise,” and 1 Corinthians 15:33 warns, “Bad company corrupts good morals.” I no longer find my pre-Church friendships as fruitful; I’ve changed and sometimes feel out of place with my family.

Matthew 10:34: “Do not think that I have come to bring peace to the earth; I have not come to bring peace, but a sword...” Sometimes faith separates you from those you love.

## Prayer Life

Part of the required guidance for this talk was to describe how my prayer life has changed. That one’s easy: I now have a prayer life. What was once absence is now a daily habit and source of direction.

## Witness

My life has fundamentally changed. God was the main character in the story who pursued someone who did not love him back until their eyes were opened. I am far from perfect, but I try to be a little better each day.

Here are some lessons I’ve learned in the past ten years:

- God gives just enough evidence to believe, but not so much that belief is without choice. If God appeared undeniably, we’d lose freedom of choice — and without choice there is no genuine love.
- Hebrews 11:6: “But without faith it is impossible to please him, for anyone who approaches God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him.”
- God hides enough to keep our hearts honest and reveals enough to draw us closer.

When people ask how I can believe in a God who allows suffering, I answer that God allows freedom because without it love is impossible. The Sword of Truth series illustrated this for me: a world where everyone is compelled to be kind looks peaceful but empty; freedom of choice is essential to authentic love.

I used to see churchgoers as hypocrites; now I see sinners striving to be better. I tell leaders I mentor there are three realities: who you aspire to be, who you perceive yourself to be, and how others perceive you. The goal is to close the gaps between those realities. God alone knows the true you, and He alone transforms the heart at its core.
